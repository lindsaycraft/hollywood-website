The Star System: How Old Hollywood Owned Its Actors

The Seven-Year Contract

Studios locked contract players into "option contracts" that could run as long as seven years. Every six months, the studio alone decided whether to renew, while the actor had no power to leave, refuse a role, or negotiate a raise. If a performer turned down a part, the studio could suspend them without pay, and that lost time was often added onto the end of the contract, stretching a "seven-year deal" into a much longer commitment. Newcomers like Clark Gable and Rita Hayworth spent years bound to a single company this way before they ever gained real leverage over their own careers.

Building the Perfect Star

Before a promising newcomer ever appeared on screen, the studio decided who that person would become. Frances Gumm was renamed Judy Garland and Margarita Cansino became Rita Hayworth, complete with new hair color, altered teeth, and a rewritten backstory. Recruits were sent to in-house "charm schools" for coaching in diction, posture, and dance so they would match the studio's vision for their image. By the time a star's first big role arrived, almost nothing about their public persona had been left to chance.

The Publicity Machine

Each studio ran its own in-house publicity department, and its job was to keep a star's image glowing in the public eye. Photographers such as MGM's George Hurrell produced glamorous portrait stills that were distributed to magazines and fan clubs across the country. Publicists arranged interviews, staged romances, and quietly buried scandals that might tarnish a star's reputation, all to protect the studio's investment. For decades, most of what audiences believed about their favorite stars came from this well-oiled machine rather than from the stars themselves.

Loan-Outs: Trading Talent Like Property

Studios routinely loaned out their contracted stars to other production companies, often charging a fee that had little to do with what the actor was actually paid. Clark Gable was on a standard MGM salary when the studio loaned him to Selznick International to play Rhett Butler in Gone with the Wind (1939), one of the most famous loan-out deals in Hollywood history. Actresses like Ava Gardner resented the practice, since they had no say in which productions they were sent to make. A loan-out could also double as a quiet punishment, sending a performer to a lesser production if they had displeased a studio executive.

The Morality Clause

The morality clause entered Hollywood contracts in 1921, when Universal Pictures began adding language that let the studio fire any performer whose off-screen conduct hurt their reputation with the public. Universal introduced the clause after the scandal surrounding actor Roscoe "Fatty" Arbuckle, and other studios soon copied the practice. During the Red Scare of the 1940s and 1950s, several studios used the same kind of clause to end the contracts of actors and writers accused of Communist sympathies, including members of the blacklisted "Hollywood Ten." What began as a way to protect a star's public image became one more tool studios could use to control contract players' lives, both on screen and off.

Bette Davis Fights Back

By 1936, Bette Davis was tired of being handed mediocre scripts despite just winning an Academy Award, so she left for England to make two films outside her contract with Warner Bros. Warner Bros. sued her in an English court, and the studio's lawyer notoriously dismissed her complaints as being about money rather than her career. She lost the case and returned to Hollywood in debt, forced to finish her contract as originally written. Even so, her public defiance made her a sympathetic figure and set an early example that other contract stars, including Olivia de Havilland, would later follow.

The De Havilland Decision

Olivia de Havilland picked up where Davis left off, suing Warner Bros. in 1943 after the studio kept adding her suspension periods onto the end of her contract. In December 1944, the California Court of Appeal ruled in her favor, deciding that the state's seven-year limit on personal service contracts meant seven calendar years, no matter how many suspensions a studio tacked on. The ruling, now known as the De Havilland Law, freed her from Warner Bros. and meant no studio could hold a performer under contract indefinitely again. It remains one of the most cited rulings in entertainment law and is widely credited with giving actors real leverage over their own careers for the first time.

The Paramount Decree and the Rise of the Freelance Star

In 1948, the Supreme Court's ruling in United States v. Paramount Pictures forced the major studios to sell off their theater chains, cutting into the profits that had funded long-term contracts in the first place. At the same time, television was pulling audiences away from movie theaters, and the studios could no longer justify the expense of keeping a large roster of stars on salary year-round. Between the late 1940s and the early 1960s, the studios gradually let their contract players go, and talent agents such as Lew Wasserman of MCA stepped in to "package" freelance stars, directors, and writers into individual projects. By the time Clark Gable's own contract lapsed in 1954, the seven-year deal that had defined Old Hollywood for three decades was already fading into history.
